Privacy Policy

Last updated: September 29, 2026

This page says what BeanPool keeps about you, where it is kept, who can see it, how long it is kept, and what you can do about it. It also says plainly what we cannot protect you from.

BeanPool is open-source software, not one company's server. Each community runs on its own server, and the person or group who runs that server (its operator) looks after that community's data. BeanPool itself runs a few shared services, listed in section 1. In this policy, "BeanPool", "we" and "us" mean the people who build BeanPool and run those services.

The short version

  • Your account is a key made on your own device. Your 12 words are the only way back into it that depends on nobody.
  • Your community's operator can read what their server holds, except what you write in direct messages, which are end-to-end encrypted. The operator can read group, event and enterprise chats, and the notices BeanPool adds to a chat about a deal, such as its amount.
  • Other members can't see your balance or your trades. If you keep an enterprise on a local community, your name and the Beans you pledge to back it are public (section 4).
  • On a local community, only its members see its listings.
  • The apps contain no analytics, advertising or crash-reporting code. The app stores give us install counts and some crash reports (section 10).
  • If you link a Google, Apple, GitHub or Facebook sign-in, a locked copy of your account is kept in BeanPool's key vault in Iceland. Opening it takes two of the three people who look after the vault, acting together, though the vault's hosting company could read it from the running vault's memory. Apart from giving it back to you, we open a copy only under a valid court order.

1. Who runs what

Communities. Each community runs on its own server. Anyone can download BeanPool's software and run a community. We can't log in to the servers of communities we don't run. Their operators, not BeanPool, decide how that data is looked after, so ask them about it first.

But if a community uses a beanpool.org address, that name is kept in BeanPool's own Cloudflare account, even for a community we don't run. Everything that comes to the community through that address passes through Cloudflare, which decrypts it on the way, whether the community is reached directly or through a tunnel. So we could point the name at another server, or read or change that traffic. If the community is reached through a Cloudflare tunnel made by our address service, that tunnel and its settings are in our account too, so we could also send requests through it to other services on that server or its network, such as another program on the machine or a home router. So could anyone who took over that account.

What BeanPool runs itself:

  • The global community at global.beanpool.org, where anyone can join with a sign-in account and try BeanPool without starting a community. We are its operator.
  • A few local communities in Australia, for which we are also the operator.
  • The key vault, which keeps sign-in recovery copies (section 3).
  • The address service, which gives communities names such as yourtown.beanpool.org.
  • The communities directory, the public map of communities on this website.
  • This website, with its newsletter sign-up and the members' guide, and the feedback service the apps send feedback to.
  • The apps, which we build and publish on Google Play and the App Store.

2. Your key and your 12 words

Your BeanPool account is a key made on your own device. It signs everything you do. Your 12 words can rebuild it.

  • On a phone, the key and your 12 words are kept in the phone's secure storage (Android Keystore or the iOS Keychain), set to stay on that phone. They are not in the phone's backups, so a new phone, even one restored from a backup, needs your 12 words or your linked sign-in account (section 3).
  • Your phone's lock. If your phone has fingerprint or face unlock set up, the app asks you to unlock before it shows your words or lets your account leave the phone. If the phone has only a PIN, pattern or password, or no lock at all, the app does not ask. While you are joining, a brand-new account can be linked to a sign-in account without being asked. You can also turn on App Lock in Settings.
  • In a web browser, the key is kept in that browser's own storage on your computer, which is not hardware-protected. Only BeanPool's own web app at global.beanpool.org keeps a key. Other communities' web pages let you look around, not sign in.
  • Your key leaves your device only when you choose: when you link another device (it is sent there encrypted to that device), or inside a locked sign-in copy (section 3). Copying your 12 words can also let them leave (below).
  • Your 12 words are the one way back that depends on nobody. Write them down on paper and keep them somewhere safe. A photo or screenshot of them can end up in your phone's cloud photo backup. The phone app blocks screenshots and screen recordings while your words are on screen. If you tap Copy, your words go on your device's clipboard. BeanPool tries to clear them a minute later, or when you come back to BeanPool if you left it first. Until then, your device may pass them to your other devices, and other apps can read them (on Android 9 or older, any app on the phone).

3. Signing in with Google, Apple, GitHub or Facebook

You can link a sign-in account so that you can get back into BeanPool if you lose your phone. On a local community this is your choice. Joining the global community without an invite needs a sign-in account, and each sign-in account can have only one account on global at a time.

What a sign-in shares. The provider tells BeanPool that it's you, with an account number and usually your email address. We use the email only to show you a shortened form of it (such as m•••@gmail.com), and we never store it. The provider learns that you signed in to BeanPool.

The locked copy. When you link a sign-in account, your app locks a copy of your account with that sign-in and sends it to BeanPool's key vault: a small server in Iceland that does nothing else. No community keeps one, not even yours. The vault keeps the locked copy; which BeanPool account it belongs to and which kind of sign-in account it is linked to; the notification addresses of your devices, so that it can warn them; two codes, made with a secret key from your sign-in account and from your BeanPool account, so that it can find your copy; and the day it last changed. It keeps no names, email addresses, IP addresses or list of your communities.

Getting back in. The vault gives your copy back only after it has checked your sign-in with Google, Apple, GitHub or Facebook itself. Every restore with a sign-in then waits 24 hours, unless a device that still has your account taps Yes, it's me. Your devices are told at once and can stop it. The vault deletes its record of a restore seven days after it ends.

Who can open the copies. BeanPool can. It takes two of the three people who look after the vault, working together, and they live in different countries. Nobody can log in to the vault. But the vault's hosting company can read the running machine's memory, which holds the keys the copies are locked with, and so could open any copy (section 15). A court could order us to open a copy (section 14). Anyone who takes over your Google, Apple, GitHub or Facebook account, including a government that makes that company help, could also get your account back, unless you stop it within the 24 hours.

If you would rather nobody but you could ever get in, keep your 12 words safe and tap Disconnect next to each sign-in account in Settings. That deletes your copy from the vault at once, and from its backups within 30 days. Your 12 words always work, with or without the vault.

Copies made by older versions. Older versions of the app kept this copy on your community's own server, where its operator could open it. The app offers to move your copy to the vault, and deletes the old one when it does. If an older version ever gave your copy back to you in a restore, the community also kept a record of what it handed out, which its operator can open too. The move doesn't delete that record: it goes when you delete your account there. On a date we announce, the communities we run delete every old copy and record still left. A community run by someone else keeps them until its operator deletes them.

The global community's record. So that each sign-in account has only one account on global, global keeps a fingerprint of the account you joined with: a code made from the provider's name and your account number with a secret key, never the number itself or your email. Anyone holding a copy of global's database could check whether a particular sign-in account has joined. If you delete your account on global, the fingerprint is erased, unless the community had removed or suspended you.

4. What your community holds, and who sees it

Your community's server holds your profile (name, picture, bio and contact details), your listings, events, polls and photos, your messages, your balance and trades, the groups you are in, the reports and blocks you make, and your settings. When a photo is stored, the server removes its location data and the camera's hidden details.

Other members see your profile, your trust badge and Trust Points, how many trades you have done, the reviews you get and who wrote each, and the listings, events and polls you share with them. They also see who invited you and whom you invited. On the global community, for a member who joined with a sign-in account instead of an invite, that shows which kind (Google, Apple, GitHub or Facebook). Your contact details are hidden until you choose who sees them.

Other members do not see your balance or your trades: who traded with whom, for what and for how much is seen only by the two people in the trade (for an enterprise, its keepers). A review shows who wrote it, so it shows that the two of you traded. A community Decision about you shows your balance to the members voting on it, inside that Decision only. A poll is anonymous: everyone sees the counts, and no member sees who voted what, unless the person who made the poll chose an open vote, which the poll says. In an open vote, members see who voted what.

People who aren't members of a local community see a sign-in page instead of its listings. Some things stay public for anyone who opens a local community's address:

  • its name and basic details, such as how many members it has;
  • its enterprises and their map pins, who keeps each one (name and picture) and the Beans each keeper has pledged to back it, and the amounts of its latest payments in and out, without their notes;
  • its crowdfunds and Commons projects, with who started each;
  • its community Decisions, which can name a member, for example in a vote to suspend someone;
  • its Pulse feed: members' public posts from other platforms, with their name and picture;
  • members' profile pictures, and the name that goes with any member's key;
  • whether a name is in use there. To help someone get back into their account, it also lists the members whose names begin with the letters typed, with their picture and the date they joined, but only members who still have an old sign-in copy kept on that community (section 3);
  • a listing's photos, to anyone who has their link. Only members' apps are given the links.

On the global community, visitors see only listings (with their photos, a rough area and no names), the community's basic details, and whether a name is in use. Nothing else about its members is public there.

Your community's operator can read everything the server holds, except the content of your direct messages. That includes group, event and enterprise chats, every balance and trade, who reported or blocked whom, and how each member voted in polls and Decisions (the server records each vote so that each member counts once). Unless an old sign-in copy is still kept there (section 3), the operator has no way to the key on your phone or to your 12 words. A web browser is different. On the global community, the web app's code comes from our server, so we, as its operator, could change that code to read the key of someone using it there (section 15). Other communities' web pages keep no key.

A standby. Some communities keep a second server ready to take over if the first one fails. It copies nearly everything above, and whoever runs it can read that copy.

The hosting company that rents out a community's server can reach its disk and its memory, as with any rented server.

Messages. Direct messages between two people, and the photos in them, are end-to-end encrypted: the server stores them in a form only the two of you can read. If the app can't lock a message this way, it doesn't send it, and the server refuses a direct message that isn't locked. The server does know who wrote to whom and when, and it sees emoji reactions. The operator can also read the notices BeanPool itself adds to a chat about a deal (Beans held, paid out or given back when a deal is cancelled, the amount, and how a stuck trade was settled and why), a message the operator sends you from the server, and any message an older version of the app sent without locking it. Group, event and enterprise chats are not end-to-end encrypted: the server stores them in a form it can read, so the operator can read them. The phone app says so on each of those chats.

Linked communities. Communities can link with each other, but linking is switched off unless an operator turns it on. A listing stays in your own community unless you choose to widen its reach.

5. Location

BeanPool does not track your location. There is no background location tracking of any kind. The phone apps ask for your location only while you are using them.

Location exists in BeanPool only as a pin you place yourself, on a listing, an enterprise or an event. You can use your device's location once, at the moment you place the pin, or drop the pin by hand anywhere on the map. A listing's pin is rounded to about 11 metres. An enterprise or event pin is saved where you put it, unless you mark it Approximate, which rounds it to about 100 metres before it is saved, so a pin near home need not be your front door.

  • Listing and event pins are seen by your community's members. On the global community, visitors see only a rough area.
  • Enterprise pins can be seen by anyone who opens a local community's map. On the global community, only its members see them.
  • Your rough area, if you choose to share it, is rounded to about 11 km before it is stored. Other people see only how many kilometres away you are. Places you ask to be alerted about are stored rounded the same way.

Place a pin where you are willing to be found, and use Approximate when it is at someone's house.

6. Notifications

If you allow notifications, your phone gives the app a notification address, and your community's server keeps it. Notifications go from the server through Expo's notification service, then Apple or Google, to your phone. Each carries a short line, such as who sent you a message, the name of a group, or which listing a deal is about and its amount in Beans. An announcement from your community's operator carries its whole text. A notification never carries the text of a message. Expo, and Apple or Google, see what each notification says.

You can switch most kinds off in Settings: Direct Messages, Marketplace Activity, and Held in Trust & System. Warnings that someone is getting into your account with a sign-in can't be switched off, because they are how you can stop a stranger in time.

The key vault also keeps your devices' notification addresses beside your locked copy, locked with the vault's own key rather than your sign-in, so that it can warn you about a restore.

  • Address search: when you search for a place, what you type goes to OpenStreetMap's search service (Nominatim).
  • Fonts: the web app loads its font from Google Fonts.

  • Video players: a Pulse video plays in its platform's own player, such as YouTube or TikTok, which follows that platform's own privacy policy.
  • The members' guide in the apps is loaded from this website.

8. The Pulse: channels you connect (TikTok, Instagram and video feeds)

Members may optionally connect their external publishing accounts (such as TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, SoundCloud, or RSS feeds) to syndicate their public posts into the community Pulse feed. Connecting a platform is entirely voluntary and member-initiated.

When connecting an account via OAuth (TikTok or Instagram):

  • Device-Side Token Storage: You authorize your account directly with the platform provider. Platform OAuth access and refresh tokens are stored only on your own device, in its secure storage. On an iPhone, the phone's own backups, such as iCloud Backup, can include them. Access tokens are never stored on or held by the community node database.
  • Limited Scope & No Private Data Access: BeanPool requests only the basic public profile permissions needed to verify channel ownership (such as username and public identifier) and list public videos or posts. The system does not access, read, or store your private messages, direct messages (DMs), follower or following lists, or private/unlisted content.
  • What the Node Stores: When public posts are syndicated to the feed, the community node stores only public post metadata: the platform name, your channel identifier and member public key, the platform post identifier, the canonical post URL, the public post title or caption snippet, the preview thumbnail image URL, the publication timestamp, and your selected category. The node also keeps a cached copy of each post's preview image, described below.
  • Preview Images Only — No Video or Audio Hosting: So that the feed loads on slow connections, and keeps working after a platform's image link expires, the community node fetches each post's preview thumbnail image and keeps a cached copy, which it can serve to the feed itself. Only still images are cached (JPEG, PNG, WebP, GIF or AVIF, at most 2 MB each), in memory (up to 20 MB, cleared when the node restarts) and on the node's disk (up to 100 MB in total, with the least recently viewed images removed first when that limit is reached). Cached images are not copied to other nodes. Both the web app and the phone app load preview images through the node, so your device does not contact the platform's image servers when it shows a preview; if the node cannot supply an image, the card shows a plain platform placeholder instead. Video and audio are never downloaded, copied, or re-hosted on the community node: content is played through each platform's official player or opened via an external link.
  • Retention & Scrubbing: The node keeps the 20 most recently published items from each connected channel. Items beyond those 20 are automatically pruned, typically within minutes of newer posts arriving; items are not removed on age alone. Curated learning content that BeanPool supplies to every node is exempt from this limit and contains no member data. When an item is pruned or deleted, the node records a tombstone deletion timestamp and immediately scrubs the post URL, title, and thumbnail URL to NULL, erasing that data from the database. From that moment the node stops serving the item's cached preview image. The cached image goes with the item on every path that removes it — deleting a single post, deleting a channel, deleting your account, the automatic retention pruning, the inactivity prune, and an operator takedown — and is deleted from the node's memory and disk at that moment, not on its next request.
  • Disconnecting & Deleting Channels: You can disconnect an OAuth account at any time in the app, which immediately deletes the stored access token from your device and clears verification on the node (existing imported items remain until you delete them or the channel, or until newer posts from the same channel push them out of the 20 kept). If you delete a channel entirely, the channel link and handle are immediately scrubbed to NULL and all associated imported feed items are scrubbed from the node.

9. Events, RSVPs and event chat

Members can post an event to their own community or to one of its groups. The community's server stores what you type into the event — its title and description, the start and end times, the place name, the map pin you drop, any photos, and the private note for people going — together with each RSVP: which member marked themselves Going or Interested, and when. The host sees who has replied. Members marked Going can see each other in the event chat. Everyone else sees only the counts.

  • The private note: shown only to the host and to members marked Going. It is never part of a public listing.
  • The event chat: open to the host and everyone marked Going. It is not end-to-end encrypted, unlike a direct message: the server stores these messages in a form it can read, so that the host can remove a message and so that membership can follow the RSVPs. Your community's operator can read an event chat. The app says so on the chat screen.
  • It stays in your community: an event, its RSVPs, the private note and the chat are never sent to another community. They stay on your community's own server, its standby if it has one, and its backups.
  • Thirty days after the event ends: the RSVPs, the chat messages and the private note are deleted from the server, and from its standby. The event itself remains, inactive, as other finished posts do.

10. What BeanPool itself holds

  • The global community and the communities we run: everything in section 4, for their members, because we are their operator. Beans are switched off on the global community.
  • The key vault: the locked copies described in section 3.
  • The address service: each community's name, its server's public key, how the server is reached (and its IP address, when it is reached directly), and a contact for its operator if they give one. Nothing about members. Every beanpool.org name, and every tunnel the service makes, is in BeanPool's Cloudflare account (section 1).
  • The communities directory: a community's server lists itself there unless its operator switches that off. The global community doesn't list itself. For each community listed: its name and web address; unless its operator switches each off, the area it serves (a point on the map and a distance around it), its member count, and its software version and whether it is online; and its contact email and phone number only if its operator turns each on. The directory is public: it is shown on this website's map and in the global community.
  • The newsletter: your email address, if you sign up on this website. Signing up also makes an account for that address in Supabase's sign-in service, and you get an email to confirm it. Once you confirm, Supabase's records keep the IP address and browser you confirmed from and, each time you come back to this website in that browser, the IP address and browser of that visit; Supabase's logs of that service record each request with the IP address it came from.
  • Feedback: the text you send, its kind, the app version, platform and language, and your community's name if you type it. Not your IP address, your account or your community's address. We may copy feedback into an issue or discussion on GitHub, which may be public, so please put nothing private in it.
  • The app stores: Google Play and the App Store tell us how many people install the apps. From phones whose owners chose to share diagnostics with Google or Apple, they also give us crash reports: the phone's model, its system version and where in the app it failed, with no name. This comes from the stores, not from code in the apps.
  • This website loads its fonts from Google Fonts, some of its code from unpkg.com and jsdelivr.net, and its map pictures from OpenStreetMap. The directory map and the newsletter sign-up talk to Supabase.

11. Where it is kept

  • Australia: the communities we run, at Binary Lane (an Australian company); the communities directory and the newsletter list, at Supabase (a US company), in its Sydney region.
  • United States: the global community, at DigitalOcean in New York; notifications pass through Expo; feedback we copy to GitHub; the app stores' install counts and crash reports, at Google and Apple.
  • Cloudflare (a US company with servers around the world): the address service, the feedback service and this website, and, in BeanPool's Cloudflare account, every beanpool.org name and every tunnel the address service makes, including those of communities we don't run. Every community we run, including global, and every community that uses a beanpool.org address, directly or through a tunnel, is reached through Cloudflare, which decrypts its traffic at Cloudflare's servers before passing it on. The global community's photos are stored with Cloudflare.
  • Iceland: the key vault, at an Icelandic hosting company. Its encrypted backups are kept by a second provider in another country, which cannot read them.
  • Communities run by others: wherever their operator chose.

12. How long it is kept

  • Your account at a community: until you delete it (section 13). A community's server makes a snapshot of its database every day and keeps the last seven, unless its operator changes that.
  • Event RSVPs, chat and private note: 30 days after the event ends.
  • A request to join a community (a knock): what you wrote is cleared once it is answered or lapses, and the record is deleted 30 days later.
  • Pulse items: the 20 newest from each channel.
  • Your IP address: a community's server uses it to limit how often something can be done, and keeps it only in memory, with these exceptions:
    • to limit sign-ups and requests to join, it keeps a code made from your address with a secret key, for one day;
    • it records the address its standby copies from, and the address of anyone who asks for a copy of its database without being allowed, keeping the latest 20 in its settings, which are part of its snapshots and backups;
    • its log records the address of anyone who types a wrong admin password several times, and of a server that passes on other people's traffic without being set up as trusted.
    These recorded addresses are deleted after 30 days. The feedback service keeps a code made from your address for one day. Cloudflare sees the IP address of every visit that passes through it (section 11).
  • Community servers' logs: the newest 2,500 entries, plus the server's own output, capped at 30 MB.
  • The key vault: your copy until you tap Disconnect, or link a different BeanPool account to the same sign-in account; encrypted backups for 30 days; after you Disconnect, a record of the deletion (the two codes and the day) for 30 days, so that a vault rebuilt from an older backup deletes your copy again; the record of a restore for seven days after it ends; its error log for seven days, with nothing about requests in it.
  • The global community's fingerprint of your sign-in account: while your account there exists, and after that if the community had removed or suspended you (section 3).
  • Feedback: until we delete it. There is no fixed schedule yet.
  • The newsletter: your email address, and the sign-in account Supabase keeps for it with the IP address and browser you confirmed from or last visited this website from, until you ask us to remove them. Supabase deletes its logs on its own schedule.

13. Your choices

  • 12 words only, or a linked sign-in account (sections 2 and 3). Disconnect removes a sign-in account and its copy.
  • Who sees your contact details, in your profile settings.
  • Approximate pins, and whether to share your rough area (section 5).
  • Which notifications you get (section 6).
  • Sign out takes your account off this device only.
  • Delete your account: in Settings, under Account Deletion & Sign Out, tap Permanently Delete Account. It deletes your account at the community you are using, and takes your account off this phone for all your communities. Your profile is erased (your name becomes "Deleted Member"), with your picture, bio, contact details and rough area. Your listings and the events you host are cancelled, and your open polls are closed. Your notification addresses, place alerts, blocks, friends, settings, Pulse channels, and any sign-in copy held by that community and its record of restores, are deleted. If you keep an enterprise, you stop keeping it and your pledge to it is settled. Any balance is settled with the community's Commons pool. You can't delete an account while one of your deals is in escrow.
  • What stays after you delete:
    • the ledger entries of your trades and crowdfund pledges (amounts, dates and any note on them), because other members' balances depend on them;
    • messages you sent, in direct messages and in group, event and enterprise chats;
    • ratings you gave and got, reports you made, and reports other members made about you;
    • your votes in polls and in community Decisions;
    • crowdfunds and Commons projects you started, and Decisions you proposed, with what you wrote in them. On a local community they stay public (section 4);
    • the text, photos and pins of your cancelled listings and events, and your closed polls;
    • the groups you were in, including a group you led, which then has no lead;
    • your replies to events, until 30 days after each event;
    • the invites you made, and who invited you and whom you invited;
    • smaller records of what you did there, such as your votes for a group's or an enterprise's lead, your price reports, and a record of any move to a new key; the community's activity feed, until it is cleared after 30 days; and your name as a visitor at linked communities you traded or messaged with, or that showed one of your listings;
    • copies of the server's database made before you deleted, with your whole profile in them: its daily snapshots, until they are replaced (a week, unless the operator keeps more); any backup the operator made, for as long as they keep it; and a standby's copy, if the standby has stopped copying.
    They are shown under "Deleted Member" instead of your name, but anything written in them stays as it was written.
  • Deleting is per community. It does not delete your account at your other communities. Because deleting takes your account off this phone for all your communities, delete it at your other communities first, or keep your 12 words to get back in and do it. It does not delete your copy in the key vault: tap Disconnect next to each sign-in account first.
  • Ask us: for anything BeanPool holds, email admin@beanpool.org. For a community run by someone else, ask its operator. If you can't reach them, email us and we will pass your request on. See also Data Deletion.

14. Requests from the authorities

The key vault. We open a copy only under a valid court order, and we tell the member unless we are forbidden to. Opening one takes two of the vault's three custodians, who live in different countries. Once a year we publish how many requests we received.

Everything else BeanPool holds (section 10): we comply with valid legal orders. A community run by someone else answers to its own operator and the law where they are.

Companies we use can be ordered directly in their own countries, without asking us: the hosting companies for the servers they host, and Cloudflare for the traffic it carries.

For child safety, see our Child Safety page.

15. Security, honestly

What protects you: your key is made and kept on your device; direct messages are end-to-end encrypted; photos lose their location data; opening a vault copy takes two people in different countries; a break-in at one community we run does not give control of the machine it shares with the others; and no community we run holds a key that could change another community's address.

What we cannot protect you from:

  • Your community's operator, and whoever runs its standby, can read what their server holds (section 4).
  • A hosting company can read the disk and memory of a server it hosts. That includes the key vault's host, which can read the running vault's memory, and so could open any copy.
  • Communities we run on the same machine can still reach each other's servers over a network they share inside it, so one that was broken into could get round another's limits on repeated attempts.
  • Cloudflare decrypts the traffic of every community reached through it (section 11).
  • Whoever controls BeanPool's Cloudflare account could point any beanpool.org name at another server, read or change the traffic of any community that uses a beanpool.org address, send requests through a tunnel our address service made to other services on that server or its network, and change the global community's web app on its way to you (section 1).
  • Anyone who takes over your Google, Apple, GitHub or Facebook account could get your account back through the vault, unless you stop it within the 24 hours. Protect that account with its own strong password and two-step login.
  • Two of the vault's custodians together, or a court that makes two of them act, can open any copy.
  • Whoever can publish BeanPool's apps or server software could ship a version that reads your key. The phone apps change only through a new version in Google Play or the App Store. The web app's code comes from the global community's server, which we run, so we, or anyone who took over that server, could change it to read the key of someone using it. The phone app is the safer place for your key.

16. Children

BeanPool is meant for adults. It does not ask your age. If you believe a child has an account, report it in the app, or email safety@beanpool.org. See our Child Safety page.

17. Changes to this policy

When we change this policy, we change the date at the top. Earlier versions are kept in BeanPool's code repository on GitHub.
